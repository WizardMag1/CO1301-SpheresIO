--- slide 3 ---
Introduction
Video games are a global form of Entertainment and an Art Medium.
Hence, they need a form of Regulation.
Regulation occurs via:
Censorship: modifying or restricting content.
Rating Systems: providing age-appropriate classification.
Both are meant to balance freedom of expression with the protection of the audience.
--- slide 4 ---
What is Censorship in Games?
 Limitation or modification of content by:
 Governments (through laws and bans)
 Publishers/Platforms (self-censorship)
 Culture of Political Institutions
 Commonly censored elements:
 Extreme violence 
 Sexual or nudity content
 Political/ideological references
 Religious symbolism
 Drugs or gambling themes
--- slide 5 ---
Some Examples of Censorship Worldwide
Germany (EU)
Strict restrictions on Nazi symbols until 2018
(e.g.) Wolfenstein edited to remove swastikas
United Kingdom
Some games refused classification 
(e.g.) Manhunt 2 was initially banned in 2007 for violence
China
Bans politically sensitive or supernatural content 
(e.g.) PUBG – rebranded as a Game of Peace
Australia
Games like Fallout 3 were censored due to drug use themes
--- slide 6 ---
But why does censorship happen? (Purpose)
 Protect vulnerable groups (esp. minors) from harmful content
 Help parents/guardians make informed decisions.
 Comply with national laws (maintain legal compliance)
 Maintain cultural and moral standards
 Control political narratives
 Ensure games can be legally sold in different markets
 Balance: Creative freedom and social responsibility
--- slide 7 ---
So, what are rating systems?
Age-based classification tools for parents, players, and retailers
Provide guidance (but not always legal restrictions)
Use age categories and content descriptors
Vary by region but share the same goal
To help the customers make informed choices
--- slide 8 ---
Major Rating Systems (Global)
PEGI (EU and UK)
Ratings: 3, 7, 12, 16, 18
ESRB (North America)
Ratings: eC (early childhood), E (everyone), E+10 (everyone 10+), T (teen [13+]), M (mature [17+]), AO (adults only [18+])
CERO (JAPAN)
Ratings: A (all), B (12+), C (15+), D (17+), Z (18+)
Other Systems
USK (Germany)
ACB (Australia)
IARC (Digital global systems)
--- slide 9 ---
PEGI Ratings System
Pan European Game Information (PEGI)
The system was established in 2003
Adopted by most EU countries + UK
Age categories: 3, 7, 12, 16, 18
Includes content descriptors 
The UK is a participant in the PEGI system. 
Cyprus officially supports PEGI, but there is no specific legislative basis
No age or content rating system in place and no related legislation.

--- slide 11 ---
BBFC (UK)
British Board of Film Classification (BBFC)
The system was established in 1912, 
Originally for films, but historically responsible for classifying video games in the UK before 2012
Focused on: violence, sex, language, horror, drugs
The system was abandoned in 2012 for games and replaced with PEGI
--- slide 12 ---
ESRB Rating System
Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB)
Founded in 1994 by the Entertainment Software Association (ESA)
Mainly used in the USA, Canada, and Mexico
Different kinds of censorship in the UK and the rest of Europe.

How Ratings Work
Publishers submit game footage and a questionnaire
Review panels analyze:
Violence intensity and realism
Language used (mild to strong)
Sexual content and nudity
Drugs, alcohol, and gambling
Frightening or horror elements
Outcome: age label and descriptors
--- slide 15 ---
Inconsistencies and Other Issues
Sims 4 was Rated as “T” by ESRB, 12 by PEGI, 6+ by USK, and 18+ by RARS.
Mass Effect was rated 12 by BBFC and 18 by PEGI due to a controversial side mission, including a sex scene.
Sexy Poker was rated 15 by the BBFC and 12 by PEGI, even though it uses nudity as an incentive/reward.
Manhunt 2 refused a rating by the BBFC, but ESRB rated the game as AO (Adults Only). 
--- slide 16 ---
Criticism and Challenges
Censorship issues:
Limits artistic expression
Inconsistent across countries
Rating Issues:
Not always enforced (especially in digital platforms)
Different standards in different regions
Online games usually bypass age checks
Examples: same games may be PEGI 18 in the EU, but M(17+) is US
--- slide 17 ---
Conclusion
Censorship and ratings deeply affect game development and distribution
EU &amp; UK use PEGI while other regions use other systems.
Censorship varies - what’s banned in one country may be fine in another.
Core tension: player protection vs freedom of expression
Future: Unknown due to global harmonization (including online gambling and AI-generated content)
